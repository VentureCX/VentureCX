# Hudson's permitted farmhouse leads a thin field

**Bottom line:** Columbia County near Hudson is the best region for this plan, and **725 Route 66, Hudson/Claverack ($680K, 12.82 acres, 1850 4BR farmhouse, original dairy barn, listed as an already "fully permitted" Airbnb, about 10 minutes from Hudson Amtrak)** is the single best opportunity found. The runner-up is **299 Stump Pond Rd, Livingston Manor, Sullivan County (about $444.5K, 23.4 acres, two vintage farmhouses, a large barn needing repair)**. It is the cheapest route to two rentable dwellings in a strong STR market, but it carries the most renovation and status risk. None of the top-three properties, including the Pine Plains benchmark at 7668 Route 82, breaks even at 25% or 35% down. Financed, they run **about $20–40K a year short** at local-average STR revenue. Bought for cash, each one roughly covers its own taxes, insurance and operating costs, with a few thousand dollars to spare. At 25% down, breaking even needs **$77–109K of STR gross a year**, about 1.8–2.1x what an average listing earns in these towns. The cheaper northern and western regions (Delaware/Schoharie/Otsego, Washington/Rensselaer) sell land at $2–4.5K an acre, a fifth of Columbia prices, but their STRs gross only **$18–38K**. They also sit near or beyond the 3-hour stretch limit, so cheap land does not fix the gap. **No listing status could be verified on November 17, 2026**, because portals blocked direct fetches. Only one listing in the whole study, 3880 County Route 30 in Salem, was confirmed active, and it ranks fifth. Treat every property below as a lead to confirm by phone. This is general research, not financial advice.

## Revenue per listing, not price per acre, separates the regions

A mortgage is paid from revenue, so the close-in markets win, even though acreage costs several times more there. ## Town permit rules now decide which farms can legally host guests

Every region now layers county taxes on top of state sales tax. Since March 2025, New York sales tax has applied to all STR stays ([NYS Tax Dept](https://tax.ny.gov/pubs_and_bulls/publications/sales/short-term-rental.htm)). County occupancy taxes run 2% in Delaware, 4% in Ulster, Greene and Washington, 5% in Dutchess and 6% in Otsego ([FreshAir NY](https://freshairny.com/blog/2025-guide-hudson-valley-catskills-str-regulations); [Berardi Realty](https://berardirealty.com/blog/greene-countys-short-term-rental-rules-arent-one-law-theyre-three-different-bets); [Glens Falls Chronicle](https://www.glensfallschronicle.com/?p=64567); [Dutchess Finance](https://www.dutchessny.gov/Departments/Finance/Hotel-Occupancy-Tax.htm); [BNBCalc](https://www.bnbcalc.com/blog/short-term-rental-regulation/Oneonta-New-York-Guide)). Guests pay these taxes. The town rules are what decide whether a farm can rent at all.

**Columbia** requires anyone renting for under 30 days to register with the county. Town permits do not replace county registration. Airbnb has remitted the county tax since April 1, 2026 ([Columbia County](https://www.columbiacountyny.gov/611/Occupancy-Short-Term-Rentals)). Rules for Ghent, Claverack, Hillsdale, Copake, Chatham and Austerlitz could not be found online, which makes the town clerk the first call. Germantown's drafts floated a one-rental-per-owner limit ([Germantown hearing](https://germantownny.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/2023-07-25-tbpubhearing.pdf)). In Greene, the Town of Hunter caps permits at 250, and Windham withdrew its draft law after a petition ([Berardi Realty](https://berardirealty.com/blog/greene-countys-short-term-rental-rules-arent-one-law-theyre-three-different-bets)).

**Ulster is closing to non-owner-occupied STRs.** Haus reports that the Town of Rochester, which covers most Accord addresses, set its **2027 cap on new non-owner-occupied permits at zero**. Olive's cap is full for 2026 with a waiting list. Marbletown allows one owner-occupied plus one non-owner-occupied permit per owner, and a 2025 law restricts STRs on properties with an ADU ([Haus](https://www.str.haus/blog/ulster-county-ny-short-term-rental-regulation-by-town/)). The Rochester claim rests on that single source and needs confirming with the town clerk. Sullivan is lighter: Callicoon (since 2016) and Delaware (since 2019) require a permit and inspection with no cap ([River Reporter](https://www.riverreporter.com/stories/the-blessings-and-blights-of-short-term-rentals,62940)). The trend is tightening, though. The Village of Monticello banned stays under 30 days in February 2026 ([Sullivan County Democrat](https://www.scdemocratonline.com/stories/monticello-bars-short-term-rentals,224551)). The Town of Rockland, which governs Livingston Manor, was not researched.

**Delaware County** is moving to registration. Roxbury's draft law is modeled on Andes and Margaretville ([The Reporter](https://www.the-reporter.net/stories/county-planning-board-urges-caution-as-roxbury-weighs-short-term-rental-law,255557)), and Middletown holds a hearing on December 2, 2026 ([Town of Middletown](https://www.townofmiddletowndelcony.gov/)). One guide claims Andes requires the owner to be present overnight, which would bar whole-home rentals. That claim is unverified ([STR Profit Map](https://www.strprofitmap.com/regulations/NY/andes)). Delaware's watershed towns add a second layer. NYC DEP reviews new or repaired septic systems and construction near streams, and certain work within 100 feet of a watercourse needs a stormwater plan ([NYC DEP](https://www.nyc.gov/site/dep/environment/regulations.page)). That makes converting a barn into a second unit slower in Bovina, Andes or Roxbury. **Washington/Rensselaer** is the least regulated region: Fort Ann is the only WashCo town with its own registry ([Glens Falls Chronicle](https://glensfallschronicle.com/?p=59633)). **Pine Plains** has no STR law yet. Its zoning committee has said it wants to "discourage the purchase of investor units," and it has floated a primary-residence test ([New Pine Plains Herald](https://www.newpineplainsherald.org/pine-plains-opens-the-door-to-short-term-rental-regulations/)).

The pattern favors a buyer who lives on the farm and rents a second dwelling, or the main house while living in the second. Owner-occupied permits stay uncapped in Rochester and Olive, and Pine Plains has signaled tolerance for hosted rentals. Investor-style whole-house rentals are where caps bite. A property that already holds a permit, as 725 Route 66 claims to, has real value, but only if the permit transfers to a new owner. Esopus, for one, explicitly bars transfers on sale ([Haus](https://www.str.haus/blog/ulster-county-ny-short-term-rental-regulation-by-town/)).

## No top-three farm breaks even at 25–35% down

The model follows the earlier Pine Plains method. **STR net before property costs = 68.5% of gross minus $13K of fixed costs.** The 31.5% variable cost covers the 15.5% Airbnb host fee and roughly 16% for cleaning, and the $13K covers utilities, maintenance and software, assuming self-management. Insurance is $3.5K, taxes come from listings or estimates, and the farm is treated as breaking even in the early years. Debt is a 30-year loan at 7.25%, with i = 0.0725/12 and n = 360. The payment factor is i(1+i)^360 / ((1+i)^360 − 1) = **0.0068218 per dollar per month, or 0.081861 per year**. The model excludes closing costs, startup furnishing and farm capex, a house reserve and the owner's labor, so it flatters every case.

The sensitivities show where each property's risk sits. **725 Route 66** at $40K gross falls to +$1.9K bought for cash and −$39.8K at 25% down. At $60K gross, which is plausible if its existing reviews are strong, it reaches +$15.6K for cash and −$26.1K at 25% down. **Pine Plains** at a conservative $42K gross barely covers its costs for cash (+$0.3K), and at an upside $62K it loses $32K a year at 25% down. **Stump Pond** is the only property where a realistic configuration approaches break-even with financing. If both houses are rented, the model assumes $76K gross and $26K of fixed costs for two units, which leaves −$13.7K a year at 25% down and −$10.1K at 35%. That case leaves the owner living elsewhere. It also excludes renovation, which could easily run into six figures for two vintage houses and a barn. If renovation is paid in cash, Stump Pond at 25% down needs about $111K plus renovation. That is roughly the cash 725 Route 66 needs at 35% down, and Stump Pond's gap is smaller. The difference is execution risk.

The two other ranked properties fare worse. 204 George Rd at $48K gross loses $45.2K at 25% down. Salem's two units at an assumed $40K combined lose $55.6K, and even bought for cash Salem runs $9.5K short, because its $13,410 tax bill overwhelms a $20K-per-unit market. Cheap land with weak revenue does not break even even with no debt at all.

Two levers can narrow the gap, and neither closes it. The first is the agricultural assessment. It can cut tax on qualifying farmland, though never on the house and homesite. It requires $10,000 or more of average gross farm sales, or a written lease of five years or more to a qualifying farmer ([NYS DTF](https://www.tax.ny.gov/research/property/assess/valuation/ag_overview.htm)). On small homestead parcels like these, most of the assessment sits in the house, so the savings are a few hundred to a few thousand dollars. The second is a second rentable unit, which is why Stump Pond ranks second and why 725 Route 66's barn, described as suited to studio conversion, is the upside case there. Check town ADU rules first: Marbletown and Stanford both restrict STRs in ADUs ([Haus](https://www.str.haus/blog/ulster-county-ny-short-term-rental-regulation-by-town/)). Financing also constrains the deal structure. Fannie Mae does not buy loans on agricultural-type properties ([Fannie Mae B4-1.3-04](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b4-1.3-04/site-section-appraisal-report)). DSCR investor loans commonly cap acreage at 10–20 acres ([BiggerPockets](https://www.biggerpockets.com/forums/517/topics/1294455-what-is-the-maximum-acreage-allowed-for-a-dscr-loan)). Farm Credit East's Country Living loan "can finance any sized lot or parcel" ([Farm Credit East](https://farmcrediteast.com/en/resources/todays-harvest-Blog/a-guide-to-financing-rural-property)), but its STR policy is unpublished. Rates near 7.25% sit slightly above Freddie Mac's early-November 2026 average ([Freddie Mac](https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms)).

## Conclusion

The search changes what "best region" means for this buyer. Price per acre is a distraction: the cheapest land in the study, Delaware and Washington counties at $2–4.5K an acre, comes with STR markets that cannot cover even taxes on a $750K farm. What carries the property is revenue per listing, legal permission and a second rentable unit. That points to small, close-in homesteads near Hudson or Livingston Manor, not big acreage. 725 Route 66 is the best single bet because it starts with a permit and a revenue history to underwrite. 299 Stump Pond Rd is the runner-up because two houses on a cheap parcel is the only configuration in the study that comes within about $10–14K a year of break-even when financed. Pine Plains remains a credible fallback if it is still available, but it is not better than 725 Route 66 on any metric except its STR average.

The practical rule is that cash, not land, buys break-even. Each property covers its own costs only when debt is light. Every 10 points of down payment on a $700K farm moves annual cash flow by about $5.7K, more than a mature small farm enterprise contributes. A buyer financing 65–75% should budget a $20–40K annual subsidy as the cost of owning a working farm, or put roughly 85–90% down, the level at which base-case operating income covers the remaining loan. The timing favors moving soon: price cuts are visible in every region, and STR caps are spreading town by town. A permit that transfers, or a second dwelling the town already allows, is worth more than any extra acre. This is general research, not financial advice.
