# Medtech MRO Market Map
## Hospital Medical Equipment Service — Roll-Up & Carve-Out Opportunities

**Prepared:** September 2026
**Scope:** US maintenance, repair & overhaul (MRO) of medical equipment sold primarily into hospitals — healthcare technology management (HTM) / clinical engineering outsourcing, independent service organizations (ISOs), and modality-specific repair verticals.

---

## 1. The View (Executive Summary)

**The thesis works, but not where a first look suggests.** The US medical equipment service pool is roughly **$22B** (range $18–26B), of which the hospital-facing serviceable market is **$13–16B**. It is genuinely fragmented — the FDA's own 2018 estimate counted up to **~21,000 entities** servicing medical devices in the US, and no better census exists — and the demand backdrop is strong: hospitals at ~1% median operating margins are converting from in-house to outsourced maintenance (**~48% of large hospitals outsourced some biomed in 2019 → ~62% by 2025**), while a collapsing technician pipeline (~400 BMET graduates/year vs. ~7,300 annual openings) makes scaled service platforms structurally advantaged.

**Recommended posture: buy-and-build from a regional modality specialist, with carve-out optionality in years 2–3.**

1. **Anchor (months 0–12):** a $3–6M EBITDA regional ISO at 6–7x, ideally in endoscope/surgical-instrument repair or multi-modality biomed depot — ISO 13485-certified, with OEM service authorizations already in hand, in a dense Midwest/Southeast metro with acquirable neighbors.
2. **Add-ons (years 1–4):** 10–20 shops at 5–7x; return engine is the documented **1.5–2.0x multiple step-up crossing ~$10M EBITDA**, plus utilization/routing density and parts purchasing (200–500bps gross margin).
3. **Carve-out step-change (years 2–3):** the highest-conviction lane is **conglomerate-owned HTM** — Aramark exited to TRIMEDX ($300M, 2018), ABM exited to Crothall (2022), leaving **Sodexo's North America HTM** as the conspicuous remaining candidate (no confirmed sale process, but a "transition year," a CEO personally running North America, and two precedents make it the outreach to build). The largest structural prize is **IDN clinical-engineering lift-outs** on the TRIMEDX/Ascension template (~38% of large hospitals still fully insource).

**What to avoid:** OEM service-division carve-outs (no divestiture signals; service is the last thing an OEM sells), imaging field service as an anchor (OEMs hold >60% of imaging service agreements and control parts/keys), and any platform without a litigation reserve — **Avante Health Solutions won its right-to-repair jury verdict against Philips and still went bankrupt on >$20M of legal fees.** That is the sector's defining cautionary tale.

Indicative math: ~$150M invested equity building to $50M EBITDA at a ~6.5x blended entry, exiting at 10x → **~3.0–3.5x gross MOIC** before operating improvement.

## 2. Market Definition & Size

Three overlapping delivery models: **OEM service arms**, **in-house hospital clinical engineering (HTM) departments**, and **independent service organizations** (full-department outsourcers, multi-vendor service, and modality specialists).

| Measure | Estimate | Note |
|---|---|---|
| US total medical equipment maintenance spend | **~$22B** (range $18–26B) | Fortune Business Insights 2026; corroborated bottom-up from BLS technician counts (~68,000 repairers, ~$7.5–8.8B direct labor) |
| Hospital-facing SAM | **$13–16B** | Hospitals/ASCs ≈ 61% of end-user demand |
| Pure third-party (ISO) pool | **~$13B, +3.0%** (2025) | IBISWorld, narrow definition |
| Global market | $45–61B (2025), 4–15% CAGR | 35% spread across syndicated sources — treat all single-point figures as indicative |

**Provider split (working assumption):** OEM **45–55%**, in-house **25–35%**, ISO/multi-vendor **15–25%**, with ISOs gaining ~50–150bps of share per year — concentrated *outside* imaging. The market is moving to **hybrid** fleets (OEM on newest/connectivity-dependent systems, ISO on out-of-warranty multi-vendor fleets), not wholesale OEM→ISO conversion. Hybrid models are the fastest-growing provider category (~10.7% CAGR).

**Segment attractiveness for a service roll-up:**

| Segment | Size / growth | OEM lock-in | Roll-up attractiveness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Endoscope repair** | ~$3.05B global 2025 → $5.58B 2035 (6.2%); US ~$1B by 2030; ISO share growing 10%+ CAGR | Moderate (Olympus/Storz restrictive, but per-incident repair economics favor ISOs) | **Best in class** — high-frequency breakage, 25% documented hospital savings from third-party |
| **Biomed depot** (infusion pumps, monitors, telemetry, vents) | Largest by device volume; ventilator MRO alone $1.28B → $2.5B (6.9%) | Low–moderate | **Strong** — labor-led, parts available (AIV, Elite), natural in-house displacement |
| **Surgical instrument repair** | Fragmented, ~5.8% CAGR; adjacent sterile-processing outsourcing $4.6B → $8.1B (6.5%) | Low | **Strong** — Steris IMS and Agiliti anchor the top; long regional tail |
| **Ultrasound / probes** | Mature | Moderate | **Saturated** — Innovatus, Conquest, Solutech, Probo all compete; avoid as anchor |
| **Imaging field service** (MRI/CT/X-ray) | ~$8–9B US; 46.9% of maintenance market by device type | **High** — OEMs hold >60% of service agreements; no ISO >15% of parts | **Avoid as anchor** — parts/keys risk; play via parts/refurb instead |
| **Lab equipment** | ~$21B global; OEM share fell 58% → 47% (2018–25) | Falling | Adjacency — best-documented OEM-to-ISO share-shift proxy |

**Service economics:** OEM full-service contracts run ~10–15% of equipment acquisition cost per year; ISOs consistently price 20–30% below OEM (vendor-sourced claims of 30–50% on imaging). Hospital-side benchmark is cost-of-service ratio (COSR) of 5–10% of inventory acquisition value. The cleanest margin comp is **Agiliti at ~22–23% EBITDA** on ~$1.17B revenue (2023).

## 3. Why Now — Demand Drivers

1. **Hospital cost pressure is acute.** Median operating margins ~1.0% (2025) and deteriorating in early 2026; supply costs +9.9%, workforce +5.6%. **84% of HTM leaders name cost reduction as their most urgent goal** (PartsSource 2025). Service is one of the few non-clinical lines with achievable 20–30% savings.
2. **BMET labor shortage — the binding constraint and the moat.** 30+ training programs closed in five years; 23 states with no BMET pathway; ~400 graduates vs. ~7,300 openings annually; ~half the workforce over 50. A platform that can *staff* growth (in-house training academy on AAMI's registered apprenticeship model) out-competes those that can only fund it.
3. **Aging installed base.** COVID-era capex deferrals pushed replacement right (CTs averaging ~12 years, MRIs ~14 at disposition); hospital capex is being cut again in 2025–26 (~40% of executives deferring equipment spend). **Deferred capex extends equipment life and increases service intensity — service is countercyclical to capex.**
4. **Outsourcing conversion.** In-house → outsourced conversion (~48% → ~62% of large hospitals, 2019–25) is the largest organic growth source available; >75% of hospitals still retain an in-house department, so runway remains.
5. **Compliance complexity favors scale.** FDA cybersecurity requirements (§524B, June 2025 final guidance, silent on legacy devices), Joint Commission AEM/EQ103 documentation standards, and QMSR (effective Feb 2026) all raise the bar past what a two-technician shop can deliver.
6. **Right-to-repair: optionality, not base case.** FTC "Nixing the Fix" policy intent has produced **no concluded enforcement against a medical device OEM**; state laws (Colorado, Oregon) explicitly exclude medical devices; the bellwether is North Carolina H460 (Medical Equipment Right to Repair Act, 2025). The Oct 2021 DMCA §1201 exemption (renewed 2024) permits software access for repair, and FDA's 2018 FDARA report was favorable to ISOs — but parts, keys, and calibration software remain OEM-controlled in practice.

## 5. Roll-Up Thesis

**Fragmentation evidence:** FDA's 2018 FDARA §710 report estimated up to ~21,000 US servicing entities (upper bound spanning OEM/in-house/ISO — the honest framing: *the FDA itself could not count the industry*). The median independent is a $2–8M owner-operated shop; a "large" independent is $10–40M revenue. The long tail persists because shops are founder-led (ex-OEM engineers), relationship-dense, modality-specialized, and face near-zero regulatory fixed costs (pure servicers don't register with FDA). Founder demographics are now forcing succession-driven transactions.

**Observed multiples:**

| Reference | Multiple |
|---|---|
| Agiliti / THL take-private (2024) | ~9.4x adj. EBITDA |
| Sizewise add-on (2021) | ~7.7x |
| Mobile Instrument add-on (2020) | 11x 2019 adj. EBITDA |
| Medical device outsourcing sector (Capstone, 2023–25) | 12.2x (down from 14.9x in 2020–22) |
| Middle-market survey, 2026 | 6.8x typical / 9.8x premium |

**Working curve:** sub-$5M EBITDA regionals at **5–7x**; $5–15M specialists at **7–9x**; national platforms at **9–12x**. The 1.5–2.0x step-up crossing ~$10M EBITDA — not organic growth — is the primary return engine.

**Synergy stack (in order of confidence):**
1. **Technician utilization & routing density** — independents run 45–60% billable; metro overlap + central routing drops nearly fully to EBITDA.
2. **Parts purchasing** — 15–30% of COGS; aggregate spend, substitute refurbished/harvested parts, disintermediate PartsSource margin: 200–500bps gross.
3. **Cross-sell modalities into won accounts** — the biomed director is already a customer; zero CAC (Probo's and Agiliti's stated logic).
4. **National contracts / GPO access** — Vizient (>65% of acute-care providers), Premier, HealthTrust won't contract with a $6M shop; crossing national coverage unlocks a structurally unreachable segment.
5. **Training academy** — converts the sector's worst constraint into a moat (AAMI 4,000-hour registered apprenticeship template; TRIMEDX invests ~$10.8M/yr in training).
6. **ISO 13485 / accreditation umbrella** — one QMS, Joint Commission-ready AEM documentation, cyber capability across all add-ons; both cost synergy and sales weapon, and the key mitigation of FDA remanufacturing risk.

## 6. Carve-Out Opportunities (ranked)

**A. Conglomerate-owned HTM — highest conviction.** Pattern two-thirds complete: **Aramark → TRIMEDX ($300M, 2018**; 500+ facilities, 1,500+ techs ≈ ~$200K EV/technician — carve-outs price for speed, not price**)** and **ABM → Crothall (2022)**. **Sodexo NA HTM is the conspicuous remainder.** No sale process is confirmed — flag clearly — but the context: FY2025 missed revised guidance, FY2026 framed as a "transition year," the NA President retired Dec 2025, and Group CEO Thierry Delaporte personally took over North America Jan 2026 with a stated mandate to assess the portfolio. This is the single carve-out worth building proactive outreach around. (Compass/Crothall is a consolidator, not a seller.)

**B. IDN clinical-engineering lift-outs — largest structural prize.** The TRIMEDX template: a health system contributes its clinical engineering department + a 7–10 year anchor contract, takes cash plus minority equity, books immediate savings. ~38% of large hospitals remain fully insourced; health systems are demonstrably willing to JV service lines (CommonSpirit/Block, Memorial Hermann/PartsSource are recent system-as-investor datapoints). Deals are private and rarely announced — source directly against the 15–20 largest insourced IDNs.

**C. Distress / sponsor fatigue.** (i) **Avante/Staple Street** — national footprint at a $72.5M basis, debt-free, but operationally damaged; (ii) **TowerBrook/TRIMEDX continuation fund** — a sponsor that couldn't clear a sale at its mark; pieces (TRIMEDX International, Axess Ultrasound, eProtex) are plausible carve-outs; (iii) 2020–22 vintages bought at ~14.9x now marking against a 12.2x market — expect orphaned add-ons and sponsor-to-sponsor deals through 2027.

**D. OEM service/parts divisions — do not underwrite.** GE HealthCare service grew 6% and anchors its installed-base strategy; Philips divests products, defends service; no signals at Baxter/Olympus. OEM service is 40–60% gross margin and defends the equipment franchise — it is the last thing sold. Treat as optionality only.

## 7. Risks

1. **OEM access & litigation — thesis-defining.** The Avante lesson: an April 2023 jury found Philips engaged in unfair practices by withholding service keys (and cleared Avante of trade-secret misappropriation) — a landmark right-to-repair *win* — and Avante still filed Chapter 11 in Oct 2024 on >$20M of legal fees from that same case. **An ISO can win the legal argument and be destroyed by the cost of making it.** Mitigations: budget a litigation reserve; underwrite parts-cost inflation; or follow the Quality Biomedical route — *buy* OEM service authorizations through acquisition rather than fight for access. Modality-level parts/software access is a core diligence workstream: PIRG surveys show 75–83% of hospital technicians report OEM-restriction-driven downtime.
2. **FDA remanufacturing line.** Final guidance (May 2024) kept pure servicers unregulated, but the servicing/remanufacturing boundary is a flowchart, not a bright line — component-level repair, probe rebuilds, and most software changes risk reclassification into full manufacturer obligations. Mitigate with ISO 13485 and documented activity-level classification from day one. Residual risk that Congress or an adverse-event cluster reopens FDARA §710 (MITA/AdvaMed still push servicer registration).
3. **Customer concentration & hospital finances.** Losing one IDN master agreement can remove 10–20% of platform revenue; hospital consolidation (40 deals announced H1 2026) re-tenders contracts at GPO scale. Offsetting: capex deferral is service-positive.
4. **Technician labor.** Simultaneously the moat and the growth ceiling; wage inflation is structural.
5. **Integration.** Documented failure modes: generalists substituted for specialists to hit labor targets, leverage crowding out training and parts inventory, post-consolidation pricing rigidity inviting insourcing. Avante proves a six-brand roll-up can fail on integration and legal overhang despite real scale.
6. **Revenue quality caveat.** Better than typical services roll-ups (recurring, compliance-mandated, mission-critical) but worse than the backlog slide implies: per-modality terms are ~3 years, the in-house alternative is always live, and hybrid sourcing keeps competitors qualified by design.

## 8. Entry Strategies Compared

| Strategy | Entry | Pros | Cons | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Buy a mature platform** (Renovo, Probo, TRIMEDX pieces) | 9–12x, $30–80M EBITDA, $300M–1B+ equity | Immediate national coverage, GPO access, infrastructure | Multiple arbitrage already harvested (sector at 12.2x vs. Agiliti clearing at 9.4x); inherited integration debt | Only with a differentiated operating thesis |
| **B. Buy-and-build from regional ISO** | $3–6M EBITDA anchor at 6–7x (~$25–40M EV) + 10–20 add-ons at 5–7x | Full multiple arbitrage + synergy capture; $30–80M EBITDA in 3–5 yrs; ~3.0–3.5x gross MOIC on ~$150M equity | Execution-intensive; labor constraint governs pace | **Base case** |
| **C. Carve-out-first** (Sodexo HTM, IDN lift-out) | Discounted (seller optimizes for speed/balance-sheet relief) | Step-change scale; Aramark precedent ≈ $200K EV/technician | 12–24 months TSA complexity; needs an operating platform to receive it | **Years 2–3 option, not the lead** |

**Recommendation: B with C optionality.** Anchor in 9–12 months; build parts-procurement and a training academy to become a credible carve-out counterparty; pursue Sodexo HTM or an IDN lift-out as the year 2–3 step-change. Do not lead with a carve-out without a platform to receive it.

**Anchor profile checklist:** ISO 13485 certified · OEM service authorizations in hand · growth modality (endoscope/surgical instrument, biomed depot) · dense Midwest or Southeast metro with 3+ acquirable neighbors · trainable technician bench · founder seeking succession.
